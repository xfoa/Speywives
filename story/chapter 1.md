# Chapter 1

_Cauld._ The kind that seeps into your bones, needlefelted in by sharp rain. The kind that requires heat from within to vanquish. A helluva way to end a night, thought Siobhan, taking another drag of her cigarette. She peered over her arm in futile assessment: her sleeve was ripped, but there was no visible blood.

Questions and blame and guilt and anger were filtering into her head as the veil of adrenaline began to lift. I shouldae seen this coming-- no. She corrected herself: I shouldae listened to it. _I shouldae trusted myself._

She closed her eyes as she exhaled the acrid, comforting smoke, and shook her head as if these thoughts would loose themselves from it and fall off. Stupid, stupid, stupid. _Stupid._ She took another eager drag, hungry to make an already long night shorter.

There was nothing more tying her to this place tonight, and through blaring music that escaped the heavy oak doors of the club, she thought she may as well take a taxi home. After stubbing out her half menthol on the wall, she replaced what remained of it into its silver case and dug out her phone, fumbling her route home in the app on autopilot.

Naebody's reacted like _that_ tae bad news in a lang time, she thought. Cannae shoot the messenger. Especially when you paid fur the message. But the news had been particularly bad this time, and the client particularly sketchy, and the Weird was screaming out a warning she had, in a millisecond, dismissed. It was hard not to blame herself in this moment, even though she knew where the blame should rightly be placed.

The taxi landed with a dull thud, and she reflexively tapped her phone on the door. With an inoffensive _ping_ of recognition, the door unlocked and she stepped inside, the gentle slam of the closing door cutting out the distant thumps and chatter as she sat. The club and the North Bridge became small underneath, and she let out a sound, half sigh, half soft scream, now that there was no-one else around to hear her. Edinburgh streets zipped past unaware as she breathed deep, controlled breaths in the warm stillness.

_Cauld, again._ And again, a soft _ping_ unlocked her building door, unobtrusively punctuating the chorus of a million raindrops against the diminishing _sotto voce_ of the departing taxi. With each stage of the ritual -- the slam of the main door, a slow march while rummaging for her old-style physical key, resistance and click from her flat door latch -- more of the outside disappeared. The stillness from the taxi ride gradually returned, until she was left staring at her bedroom ceiling in blissful silence. She closed her eyes and fell into an instant, dreamless sleep.

_Cold._ The ice brushed against Emily's lips and she pulled back from it. Flotsam in her drink. She finished the sip anyway, and the tang of too much lime did nothing to help.

"Too much lime," she complained, but the bartender was already serving another customer. She made a face anyway, and took her caipirinha back to the table where she had her notes chaotically laid out.

_Shit!_ Some of her cocktail had sloshed over the side of her glass as she brought it down. She made a sucking sound against her teeth as she gingerly dabbed at the spilled drink with a serviette. Thankfully her writing was unharmed. What an utterly rotten evening, she sighed in her head, sitting down and getting comfortable. Or at least, as comfortable as was possible on a pub pew.

Picking up her drink (carefully, this time!) she tried to take in the jovial atmosphere around her. £5 more in the slots, she thought, and he's on his third drink. So far so good. She hadn't even worked through the first 10 lemmas, but she could _feel_ that this would converge, unlike two nights ago. Had she made a mistake? _Inconceivable_. No, that was her pride talking, of course it could be, and she'd check her work again but she knew even now she'd find nothing. What am I _missing_?

There were, of course, so many other things she could be doing on a Saturday evening in the Cowgate than drinking and mathematics. Most of of them paid better. One of them paid considerably better, had better lighting, a hundred pound buy-in, and a cute croupier named Maria, who was, at this precise hour, dealing the second shoe of the night to a table of men who thought their luck was a personality trait. She should be there. Instead she was in a pub nursing a caipirinha with too much lime, churning through the same problem for the *n*th time. It worked, it mostly worked, and that should have been fine, but there was an edge that caught every time she ran her thumb over it.

She took another sip of too-much-lime and started writing again.
